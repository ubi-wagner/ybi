# For everybody

You have an account because you did work in 2025 that somebody has to account
for, or because you hold papers that show what a cost was for. Three things
are yours: your time, your signature, and anything you can send in.

## The first five minutes

**Choose your own password.** The one you were handed is known to two people.
Until you replace it the system will not let you record anything at all — not
a timesheet entry, not a certification, not a document. It is one step and it
never comes back.

That is not bureaucracy. A judgment made on a password two people know
identifies two people, and the entire value of this system is that every
entry names one.

**You land on your own page.** What you see is built from what you have been
given to do. If a tab is not there, it is not yours — and nothing on your page
will refuse you when you click it.

**Everything you change is on the record.** Every entry, every document, every
signature names you and the moment you did it. That is the point of the
system rather than a side effect, and it is what makes your signature worth
having.

## Your timesheet

2025 is being rebuilt after the fact. Nobody kept contemporaneous timesheets,
so you are reconstructing — and a reconstruction that names its source is
evidence, while one that does not is a guess.

**Work from your records, not your memory.** A calendar, a project log, an
email trail, a shop log. The system asks which one you used, every time,
because that is the question an auditor asks.

**A week at a time is faster than a day at a time.** Most weeks look like the
week before. Fill one, then move on — nothing has to be right on the first
pass, and you can change anything until you submit.

**Over eight hours a day, or forty a week, is questioned, not refused.** Long
days happen. Say why, once, and it is accepted. What the system will not do is
let a long day pass without a word, because that is the one that gets asked
about.

**Submit when the year looks right.** Submitting is what makes your timesheet
count toward your effort rather than the reconstruction somebody else did on
your behalf.

## Certifying your effort

2 CFR 200.430(i) wants a statement from the person who did the work, about
how their time was spent. Not their manager's statement. Not the finance
office's.

**Read the distribution first.** It shows how your year's pay is spread across
the things you worked on. If it looks wrong, fix the timesheet — do not sign
it and hope.

**Only you can sign it.** The system refuses to let anybody sign on your
behalf, including the controller and the chief executive. If somebody asks you
to, the answer is that it is not possible, which is the correct answer.

**The words you sign are kept.** Not a tick box — the actual statement, stored
with your name and the date, so a reviewer can read what you attested to.

## Sending a document in

Anything that shows what a cost was for. A receipt, an invoice, a project
plan, a photograph of a machine's nameplate, a lease you were quoted.

**Send it in even if you do not know where it belongs.** Say what it relates
to in your own words. Deciding what it *proves* is a judgment that belongs to
whoever holds the portfolio for it — you are not expected to know, and a
confident wrong guess costs somebody an hour to unpick.

**You will see what became of it.** Every document you send shows as waiting
or in use. Nothing disappears into a drawer, which is where all of this was
before.

**The same document twice is one document.** It is recognised by its contents,
so there is no harm in sending something you are not sure about.

## If something looks wrong

Say so. A figure that surprises you is worth more to this engagement than a
figure that does not — the $45,000 in an intern wage account was found by
somebody noticing a number that did not look right, and it is the whole
difference between a fringe rate of 22.45% and one of 21.90%.
